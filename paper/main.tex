\begin{abstract}
Laparoscopic surgery is performed through a monocular camera that provides neither depth
nor any indication of how soft tissue is about to deform. Recent work reconstructs
endoscopic scenes in four dimensions with high fidelity, but every such system is
retrospective: it models deformation that has already occurred and cannot anticipate
deformation that has not. We present a pipeline that adds prediction to reconstruction.
Monocular video is reconstructed as a deformable Gaussian scene, a time-consistent
geometric trajectory of \numAnchors{} tracked tissue points is extracted, and that
trajectory is converted into a self-supervised dataset of \numTrainWindows{} training
windows without any clinical annotation. A conditional denoising diffusion model, trained
on that dataset and conditioned on both the observed geometry and the requested prediction
horizon, produces future geometry together with a distribution over plausible outcomes.
We evaluate against classical motion estimators and against published point-cloud
prediction methods under a single protocol, and we report two complementary metrics:
Chamfer Distance, as used in the point-cloud prediction literature, and a stricter
per-point error that the preserved point identity of our representation makes available.
We further show that the spread of the model's sampled futures correlates with its actual
error ($r=\calibCorr$), so the system provides a usable indication of when its own
predictions should not be trusted.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Minimally invasive surgery is the standard of care for a wide range of abdominal
procedures, and in the common case it is performed through a single monocular
laparoscope. That instrument provides a high-resolution view of the operative field and
nothing else: no depth, no three-dimensional structure, and no indication of how the
tissue in view will respond to the forces about to be applied to it. Surgeons compensate
through experience, and the cognitive load of doing so is associated with complications
including inadvertent tissue damage.

Computational reconstruction has advanced quickly. Neural radiance fields were applied to
deformable endoscopic tissue~\cite{wang2022endonerf}, were made substantially faster by
factored representations~\cite{ruan2023lerplane}, and have been largely superseded by
3D Gaussian Splatting~\cite{kerbl2023gaussian}, whose endoscopic
adaptations~\cite{liu2024endogaussian} achieve real-time rendering at high fidelity.

These systems share a limitation that is structural rather than incidental. All of them
are \emph{retrospective}. They answer what the tissue did, given video of it doing so.
None answers what the tissue is about to do. For intraoperative guidance the second
question is at least as important as the first, and it is the question this work
addresses.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A pipeline that extends monocular 4D surgical reconstruction with explicit
      prediction of future tissue geometry, trained entirely without clinical labels.
\item A conditional diffusion formulation over geometric displacement, conditioned on the
      prediction horizon, which also yields a distribution over plausible futures rather
      than a single estimate.
\item A single-protocol comparison against classical motion estimators and against
      published point-cloud prediction methods, reported under both a correspondence-free
      and a correspondence-aware metric.
\item An uncertainty estimate that is empirically calibrated against the model's own
      error, and an analysis of where along each recording the predictor fails.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Reconstruction of deformable surgical scenes}
EndoNeRF~\cite{wang2022endonerf} conditioned a radiance field on time to model tissue
deformation, requiring stereo input and hours of optimisation per scene.
LerPlane~\cite{ruan2023lerplane} reduced that cost by factorising the spacetime volume.
EndoGaussian~\cite{liu2024endogaussian} replaced the volumetric representation with
Gaussian primitives, reaching real-time rendering. Our reconstruction stage follows this
line and is not itself a contribution; what we add operates on its output.

\subsection{Point cloud sequence prediction}
The closest published formulation of our task is point cloud prediction.
PointRNN~\cite{fan2019pointrnn} introduced a point-based spatiotemporally-local
correlation that aggregates the previous frame's hidden states over spatial neighbours
together with the displacement to each, and applies it in place of the matrix products of
a recurrent cell. MoNet~\cite{lu2021monet} added explicit motion features;
PCPNet~\cite{luo2023pcpnet} replaced recurrence with a transformer; Mersch
\textit{et al.}~\cite{mersch2021selfsupervised} used 3D spatio-temporal convolutions on
range images. These methods target outdoor LiDAR, where point identity is \emph{not}
preserved between scans, which is why correspondence must be inferred geometrically and
why Chamfer Distance~\cite{fan2017pointset} is the standard score. We implement PointRNN
as our primary learned baseline.

\subsection{Diffusion models for forecasting}
Denoising diffusion~\cite{ho2020ddpm} with a cosine
schedule~\cite{nichol2021improved} and deterministic sampling~\cite{song2021ddim} has
become a standard generative framework. For forecasting specifically, diffusion has been
applied to pedestrian and vehicle trajectories~\cite{mao2023leapfrog,
bae2024singulartrajectory}, where its principal advantage is producing a distribution
over futures rather than a single estimate. We adopt the
v-parameterisation~\cite{salimans2022progressive} and Min-SNR loss
weighting~\cite{hang2023minsnr}, both of which matter at the very small displacement
scale of tissue motion.

\subsection{Gap}
Surgical reconstruction is retrospective; point-cloud prediction targets rigid outdoor
scenes with no correspondence and no notion of uncertainty relevant to a clinical
decision. To our knowledge no published system predicts future soft-tissue geometry from
monocular surgical video, which is the gap this work addresses.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem formulation}
Let $G_t \in \mathbb{R}^{N\times 3}$ be the positions of $N=\numAnchors{}$ tracked tissue
points at frame $t$. Given the $W=\ctxWindow{}$ most recent frames and a horizon
$k \in \{\predSteps{}\}$, we predict $G_{t+k}$. Because the reconstruction preserves the
identity of each primitive across frames, the $i$-th row of $G_t$ and of $G_{t+k}$ refer
to the same piece of tissue --- a property the LiDAR-oriented literature does not have, and
which we exploit in both the model and the evaluation.

\subsection{Predicting displacement, not position}
We predict the displacement $\Delta = G_{t+k} - G_t$ rather than the absolute position.
Predicting position directly failed by a wide margin in early experiments: generating $N$
spatially coherent coordinates from noise is a far harder problem than generating the small
correction that separates two consecutive frames.

Two scalings are applied and inverted before any metric is computed. First, each recording
is rescaled so that no high-motion sequence dominates the training signal. Second, the
target is normalised to approximately unit variance, because tissue displacement averages
$\sim\!4\times10^{-3}$ normalised units and would otherwise be destroyed by the unit-scale
noise of the diffusion process.

\subsection{Residual formulation}
\label{sec:residual}
A model that fails and emits zero reproduces whatever estimator its target is defined
against. Under a raw-displacement target that estimator is the zero-motion assumption. We
therefore define the target as the correction to constant-velocity extrapolation,
\begin{equation}
x_0 = \big(G_{t+k} - \underbrace{[\,G_t + (G_t - G_{t-1})k\,]}_{\text{constant velocity}}\big)\cdot s,
\end{equation}
so that the degenerate solution reproduces constant velocity --- an estimator that is
measurably stronger on this data --- and model capacity is spent only on what linear
extrapolation cannot express.

\subsection{Architecture}
Two pointwise encoders map the observed geometry and the noisy candidate into a latent
space. A transformer operates along the temporal axis independently per point, with the
point index folded into the batch dimension, and the most recent frame's token is taken as
the per-point summary. Two cross-attention stages then exchange information between the
observed geometry and the developing prediction in both directions, and a decoder emits
the predicted velocity of the diffusion process.

The horizon $k$ is embedded and added to \emph{both} branches. This is necessary rather
than cosmetic: the dataset pairs each context with targets at several horizons, so without
$k$ the model is asked to fit a mixture over an unobserved variable. Injecting $k$ into the
context branch in particular is what makes a velocity-scaled estimator representable at
all.

\subsection{Training and sampling}
Training follows the standard denoising objective under the
v-parameterisation~\cite{salimans2022progressive} with Min-SNR-$\gamma$ loss
weighting~\cite{hang2023minsnr} and an auxiliary term on the reconstructed clean target.
Sampling uses DDIM~\cite{song2021ddim}. Because Chamfer Distance scores a single
prediction and is minimised by the conditional mean rather than by a sample from the
conditional, we average several deterministic trajectories; the cost of doing so is
quantified in Table~\ref{tab:inference}. Setting the stochasticity parameter above zero
instead yields distinct plausible futures, whose spread forms our uncertainty estimate.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\textbf{Data.} \numVideos{} monocular laparoscopic recordings are reconstructed. Scene
geometry is initialised from monocular depth priors~\cite{yang2024depthanythingv2}, since no
stereo or depth sensor is available, and per-frame geometry is then extracted and converted
into \numTrainWindows{} training and \numValWindows{} held-out windows. The split is temporal within each recording, so every evaluated
window occurs strictly later than every training window from the same video.

\textbf{Metrics.} We report Chamfer Distance (CD), which ignores correspondence and is
therefore directly comparable with the point-cloud prediction
literature~\cite{fan2019pointrnn,lu2021monet}, and Mean Point Error (MPE), the mean
Euclidean distance between each point and its own future position. MPE is strictly harder:
a prediction reproducing the correct overall shape with every individual point displaced
scores well under CD and poorly under MPE. Both are reported in physical normalised-position
units.

\textbf{Baselines.} Classical: zero-motion, constant velocity, constant acceleration, a
least-squares linear fit over the observation window, and a two-state constant-velocity
Kalman filter. Learned: a multilayer perceptron, per-point GRU and LSTM, a deterministic
transformer of the same depth and width as our temporal encoder, and
PointRNN/PointLSTM~\cite{fan2019pointrnn}. Every learned method receives the identical
inputs, target, optimiser, schedule, batch size and epoch budget, and each is reported at
its best-validation checkpoint.

precondition for intraoperative use, and this property is independent of whether the
predictor is the most accurate method in Table~\ref{tab:comparison}.

\subsection{Temporal behaviour}
Rolling the single-step predictor forward accumulates error with a fitted exponent of
$\growthExp{}$ against the rollout step. An exponent below unity indicates sub-linear
accumulation rather than divergence: the prediction degrades but does not leave the
plausible geometry manifold.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{Scale of the quantity being predicted.} Inter-frame tissue displacement is
approximately $4\times10^{-3}$ normalised units, one to two pixels when projected. At that
scale an error in the magnitude of a predicted motion is sufficient for a method to score
no better than assuming no motion at all, even when the predicted \emph{direction} is
substantially correct. This is the dominant difficulty of the task and distinguishes it
from outdoor point-cloud prediction, where inter-frame motion is metres.

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} Model selection and reporting use the same held-out windows,
which makes the reported figures mildly optimistic. A recording held out in its entirety
would remove that bias and is the natural next step.

\textbf{Data scale.} \numVideos{} short recordings is a small corpus for a generative model,
and we observe overfitting consistent with that constraint.

\textbf{What is and is not predicted.} The model predicts tissue geometry. Instrument
motion, illumination and appearance are not modelled, so rendered comparisons isolate
geometric prediction and are additionally reported over tissue regions only.

% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a pipeline that extends monocular 4D surgical reconstruction with
prediction of future tissue geometry, trained without clinical annotation, and evaluated it
against classical estimators and published point-cloud prediction methods under a single
protocol with both correspondence-free and correspondence-aware metrics. The system
additionally provides an uncertainty estimate that correlates with its realised error.
Future work concerns a fully held-out test recording, a larger corpus, and closing the
remaining gap between predicted and true displacement magnitude identified in our analysis.
