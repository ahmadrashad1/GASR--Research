GASR · 4D Surgical Scene Reconstruction

Module 5 & 6 Explained

What the prediction system does, what every part of it is for, and what the technical words actually mean — written for a reader with no machine-learning background.

1

The whole thing in one line

A surgeon's camera shows a flat video. Our system rebuilds the scene in 3D, follows the tissue as it moves, and then tries to answer one question: where will this tissue be a moment from now?

In plain English

Think of 512 numbered stickers stuck to the tissue. We can see where every sticker was in each past frame. The predictor's job is to say where each sticker moves next.

2

What the predictor is given, and what it hands back

Before any of the internal machinery matters, it helps to see the outside of the box: three things go in, one comes out.

Why it must be told how far ahead

The same four snapshots appear in training three separate times — once paired with what happened 1 frame later, once with 2, once with 3. Without being told which one we want, the question has three different correct answers and the model cannot win. Adding that one number was a real fix, not a detail.

3

How it builds an answer: sculpting from static

This is the part called diffusion, and it is the least intuitive idea here. The model never writes an answer down in one go. It starts from pure random static and cleans it up, roughly twenty times over, each pass asking the same question: does this motion fit the four frames I was shown?

4

The change that matters most: only predict the surprise

This is the newest and most important design decision, and it needs no mathematics to understand.

There are two dumb ways to guess where a dot goes, and both are useful as yardsticks. Guess A: it stops. Guess B: it keeps going exactly as it has been. Guess B is a decent predictor of tissue — it is right about 30–57% more often than Guess A.

The everyday version

A sat-nav that assumes you keep driving straight is right most of the time. Rather than predict your whole route from scratch, it is far easier — and far safer when it is unsure — to only predict the turns.

5

Module 6: three ways to ask, and two honest yardsticks

Module 6 does not train anything. It takes the trained predictor and uses it, then grades the answers against guesses so simple that beating them should be easy.

6

What we found, and the fix it pointed to

Measuring the predicted movement against the real one separated two very different failures. The model points the right way but moves the dots the wrong distance. Direction is the hard part and it has some of it; distance is easy to correct.

7

The jargon, translated

Term you will hearWhat it actually means
Gaussian splatA small soft blob of colour in 3D. Pile up ~58,000 of them and you have a scene that can be viewed from any angle.
Anchor pointsThe 512 dots we follow. A manageable sample of the tissue rather than all 58,000 blobs.
Diffusion modelA generator that starts from random static and repeatedly cleans it up into a sensible answer. Same idea as AI image generators.
Denoising stepOne pass of that cleaning up. We use 20 of them.
HorizonHow far ahead we are asking: 1, 2 or 3 frames.
Chamfer DistanceThe score. How far, on average, the predicted dots sit from where the tissue really went. Lower is better.
Naive baselineThe "nothing moves" yardstick.
Constant velocityThe "everything keeps going straight" yardstick. Considerably better than naive on real tissue.
ResidualThe leftover bit. Here: the part of the movement that going straight fails to explain.
RolloutAsking repeatedly, feeding each answer back in, to reach further ahead than the model was trained for.
OverfittingMemorising the practice footage instead of learning the pattern, so results on new footage get worse even as training looks better.
Held-out framesFootage deliberately kept back from training, used only for marking. Without it we would be grading the model on its own revision notes.
CalibrationCorrecting the size of a prediction that already points the right way.
